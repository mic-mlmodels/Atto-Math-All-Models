After finishing my Atto Math Models project, I have uncovered some small findings that I would like to share in this short technical writeup. The project’s training pipeline expands on Deepseek-R1’s SFT → RL pipeline [Guo et al. (2025)].

## QLORA Bias Initialisation Trap

During the SFT phase of the project in training Qwen2.5-1.5B Base into Atto-Math-SFT, I recreated QLoRA by swapping the frozen model's layers for bitsandbytes linear layers and attaching from-scratch LoRA adapters. During the adapter initialisation, the original layer was assumed to have no bias as is standard practice in modern architectures due to empirical evidence discovered by [Chowdhery et al. (2022)]. However, Qwen2.5-1.5B Base utilises biases in its Q, K and V projections to “enhance the extrapolation ability of the model.” [Bai et al. (2023)], so these biases were erased after the adapters were applied. This resulted in a train and validation loss between 12-16 at initialisation, a huge increase over when pretrained biases were preserved which achieved a mean train loss of 0.4296 (4 s.f.) and a mean validation loss of 0.4773 (4 s.f.). This bugged result is even worse than sampling from a uniform distribution which would have a loss of -$$ln(1/151936) = 11.93 (4 s.f.)$$ where 151936 is the vocab size of Qwen2.5 including control tokens. This highlights the necessity of maintaining the pretrained distribution at model initialisation when running supervised fine-tuning, as seemingly small errors such as the one covered, could result in a large deviation from being in proximity to the locally optimal region of the parameter space and lead to dramatically longer or poor convergence.

## Token Repetition When Encountering Untrained Tokens

Empirically, when the input sequence includes an untrained token such as <eos> where its parameters are identical to initialisation, the model will collapse to extremely repetitive low entropy behaviours (e.g. repeating a token for >10 next-token generations). Initially this was counterintuitive as Pytorch’s nn.Embedding class is initialised with a Gaussian distribution which I predicted would result in an output distribution that's similarly high entropy. Hence, I hypothesised the model would append random tokens from its vocabulary to the sequence once there is an <eos> token. However, after reviewing theory, I conclude that this behaviour can be explained by the empirical phenomenon that attention blocks tend to skew the output distribution towards recent or repeated tokens when a clear semantic direction is missing. This is exacerbated by the greedy decoding used in the inference loop which reinforces this bias towards recent tokens by further reducing entropy and essentially traps the model in the selection of a specific token once the token has been repeated enough times [Holtzman et al. (2019)].

## Including Unverifiable Rewards to Verifiable Domains

The Group Relative Policy Optimisation in the second phase of this project relies on the MetaMathQA dataset where the reward scaling the gradient of the model is verifiable. The only return fed into the GRPO objective function is a finite horizon undiscounted return of 1 at each step of the rollout if the final answer is a direct match for the ground truth and 0 if not. While this sparse reward setup allows for ease of data collection and objective scoring for the model, the model will not improve in an episode where all answers are correct or incorrect due to GRPO’s return centering mechanism with the group mean. Hence, improved accuracy may be observed when unverifiable rewards are included in the objective function such as scoring the model’s thinking with RLAIF against a constitution [Bai et al. (2022)], adapted from the original Anthropic paper to include unverifiable math-specialised principles to incentivise coherent and efficient reasoning. This will produce a single sparse scalar reward for the sequence which can then be applied to all previous tokens as finite horizon undiscounted returns similar to the GRPO setup but nonbinary. However, this method reintroduces all the disadvantages of RLAIF including Goodharting, which is avoided by a pure verifiable approach. The model is also constrained by the judgement capability of the reward model which, if sharing the same architecture as the policy model, would be greater than the generation capability of the policy model but is nonetheless an additional constraint compared to a pure verifiable approach. Therefore, the best approach may instead be to simply gradually introduce difficult math problems into the dataset to maximise the model’s answer group distribution variance with a pure verifiable reward GRPO setup for the initial process and hone the readability and efficiency of these reasoning traces in a second phase with a reward model once the novel reasoning traces have already been discovered.
